Projector Glossary

Every term on a projector spec sheet, explained like a person instead of a datasheet. If a term is deciding your purchase, read its entry — then check your room against the calculator.

A

ALR (Ambient Light Rejecting) A screen material that rejects light coming from above and the sides while keeping the projector's image bright. The fix for living rooms with windows — paired with a bright projector it keeps the picture punchy with the lights on. Costs more than a plain white screen, but worth it if you can't control the light.
ANSI lumens The honest brightness number, measured by a standard test. Ignore "LED lumens" or "light source lumens" on the box and compare ANSI lumens only. A dark room needs far fewer than a living room with windows.

B

Black level How dark the darkest parts of the image get. This is what separates a cinematic image from a washed-out gray one, and it's the first thing cheap projectors skimp on.

C

Contrast ratio The difference between the brightest white and darkest black a projector can produce, written like 10,000:1. In a dark room, contrast matters more than brightness — it's what makes movies look expensive.

D

DLP (Digital Light Processing) The chip technology in most budget projectors. Sharp image, great motion, low input lag for gaming. Some people see "rainbow effect" — brief color flashes on high-contrast scenes. Single-chip DLP dominates the budget world.
3LCD The competing chip technology (Epson's turf). No rainbow effect and often better color brightness. Usually found in home-theater-focused models.
Dynamic iris A mechanical shutter that opens and closes to deepen blacks in dark scenes. Helps contrast on paper; in cheap implementations you can see the brightness "pumping."

E

eARC A smarter HDMI connection that sends high-quality audio (like Dolby Atmos) from your projector back to a soundbar or receiver through a single cable. If your streaming stick plugs into the projector, this is how the sound properly reaches your speakers.

G

Gain (screen gain) How reflective your screen is. 1.0 is neutral. Above 1.0 boosts brightness but narrows the viewing angle; below 1.0 (gray screens) deepens blacks at the cost of brightness. Don't chase high gain to fix a dim projector.

H

HDR (High Dynamic Range) A signal format with brighter highlights and more color detail. Budget projectors "accept" HDR but can't truly display it — they tone-map it down. Don't buy a projector for its HDR badge alone.
HDMI The cable standard everything uses. For 4K at 60Hz you want a "high speed" cable; for long ceiling runs (25 feet and up) use an active or fiber HDMI cable.

I

Input lag The delay between your controller and the screen, measured in milliseconds. Under 30ms is fine for casual gaming; competitive players want under 20ms. Game modes cut processing to hit those numbers.

K

Keystone correction Digital warping that squares up the image when the projector sits off-center. Convenient, but it softens the picture and eats pixels — physical placement (lens shift, centered mounting) always beats keystone.

L

Lamp hours How long the bulb has been running, shown in the projector's menu. Lamps dim with age — a used projector with 4,000 hours on the original lamp is living on borrowed time. Always ask the seller.
Lamp vs. laser / LED Traditional lamps are bright and cheap to replace but dim over 3,000–5,000 hours. Laser and LED light sources last 20,000–30,000 hours with no dimming, but cost more up front.
Lens shift Physically moves the lens to shift the image up, down, left, or right without moving the projector. It's optical, so there's no quality loss — the single most useful placement feature. Vertical shift is common; horizontal is rarer.
Lumens Brightness. See ANSI lumens — that's the number you compare.

O

Offset How far above or below the lens the image lands. A projector with 0% offset centered on the screen needs no tilt; high offset means it must sit well above or below the screen. Matters for shelf and ceiling mounting.

P

Pixel shifting How budget "4K" projectors fake extra resolution — the chip flashes multiple offset images fast enough that your eye blends them. Real 4K chips cost far more; good pixel shifting still looks sharp on a big screen.
Projection distance The distance from the lens to the screen. Combined with throw ratio, it decides your screen size. Measure first, buy second.

R

Refresh rate How many times per second the image updates — 60Hz is standard, 120Hz and up shows on gaming models. Higher is smoother for sports and games; movies run at 24 frames per second anyway.
Resolution Pixel count — 720p, 1080p, 4K. 1080p is the budget sweet spot; on screens under about 100 inches at normal seating distance, most people can't tell 1080p from 4K.

S

Short throw / long throw / UST Throw categories. Long throw needs a deep room (10 feet or more for a 100-inch image). Short throw sits 5–8 feet back. UST (ultra short throw) sits inches from the wall under the screen — the TV replacement, but pricier.

T

Throw ratio The key number: distance divided by image width. A 1.5:1 projector needs 1.5 feet back for every foot of image width. Check it against your room before buying anything — the calculator does this math for you.

Z

Zoom (optical vs. digital) Optical zoom moves glass — no quality loss, and it gives you placement flexibility. Digital zoom just crops the image — avoid it.
